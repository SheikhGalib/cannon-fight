\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This report documents a real-time 3-D scene, written in \textbf{C++} against
the \textbf{OpenGL 3.3 core profile}, of three medieval field cannons standing
on a grass field, aimed at a stone-and-timber fort gate.  The centre cannon
can be driven across the ground (with its wheels rolling without slipping),
its barrel can be elevated from $0^\circ$ to $45^\circ$ about the trunnion
pivot, and pressing \key{Space} fires a cannon ball that follows a
ballistic arc and breaks the bricks of the gate's two side walls.  The
whole assembly --- cannons, wheels, barrels, fort gate, trees and wooden
dummy robot --- is built from analytic primitives whose surface normals are
derived from the geometry rather than approximated, lit by a single
directional light using a per-vertex Lambert + ambient shading model in
GLSL.  The report walks the work through in three phases: (1)~the cannon
itself, (2)~the projectile and the breaking wall, (3)~replicating the
cannon, the medieval fort gate, the trees, and the dummy target robot.
Every figure is a real frame captured from the running program; every
animation loop is documented alongside the class that implements it.
\end{abstract}

%=============================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%=============================================================================

The brief was to build a scene that \emph{visibly} demonstrates a small set of
core computer-graphics ideas rather than to build a large one.  A cannon
projectile on a medieval field is a good vehicle for this because each
required concept maps onto a physical part of the object:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item the carriage \textbf{translates}, and its wheels must \textbf{rotate}
        at the rate implied by that translation, or the illusion collapses
        instantly;
  \item the barrel \textbf{rotates about a pivot that is itself carried by
        the moving carriage} --- which is exactly what a hierarchical
        transformation is for;
  \item the cannon ball follows a \textbf{ballistic trajectory} under
        gravity, and \textbf{destroys} brick walls it touches;
  \item the scene \textbf{scales}: replicating three cannons, a fort gate,
        six trees and a dummy robot introduces no new mechanism, only a
        repeated instantiation of classes built in phase 1.
\end{itemize}

The deliverable is split across three phases.  \textbf{Phase~1} builds the
cannon itself --- carriage, two wheels, barrel, elevation, lighting.
\textbf{Phase~2} adds the projectile (semi-implicit Euler integration of
gravity) and a wall of breakable bricks with sphere-vs-AABB collision
detection.  \textbf{Phase~3} replicates the cannon three times, replaces
the flat wall with a medieval fort gate (two pillars + lintel + two
breakable brick stacks flanking a doorway), places six trees behind the
fort, and adds a wooden dummy robot inside the fort as a target.

\section{Architecture}
%=============================================================================

The program is a classic single-threaded GLFW / OpenGL 3.3 core application.
A \texttt{while(!glfwWindowShouldClose)} loop in \texttt{Main.cpp} does three
things each frame:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Update} --- read input (arrows, space, esc), advance
          \texttt{Projectile}s by \texttt{deltaTime} under
          \texttt{Projectile::Gravity}, run
          \texttt{FortGate::CheckHit} against each ball, and remove
          \texttt{IsDead()} balls from the vector;
  \item \textbf{Clear} --- paint a sky-blue colour buffer and the depth
          buffer;
  \item \textbf{Draw} --- activate the GLSL shader, upload the view and
          projection matrices (which are \emph{constant} for the whole scene),
          then per-object upload the model matrix and call
          \texttt{object.Draw()}: ground plane, trees, fort gate, robot,
          three cannons, then any live projectiles.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The model matrix is \emph{not} stored anywhere --- it is recomputed every
frame from the object's \texttt{Transform} (position + rotation axis/angle +
scale), or, in the case of the wheels, from
\texttt{Carriage::MoveForward()} + \texttt{Wheel::Roll(d)} state.  This keeps
the rendering loop completely stateless about object positions: at any
moment \texttt{Cannon::GetMatrix()} returns exactly the matrix that \texttt{Draw}
will use, so a \texttt{Projectile} spawned at the muzzle gets a position that
already reflects the cannon's current drive and elevation.

\section{The mathematics}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Scene graph and model matrices}
\label{sec:hier}

Every visible object is a \texttt{Part} pair \texttt{(Mesh, glm::mat4
local)} plus a \texttt{Transform} giving the owner's \emph{parent} matrix.
The model matrix uploaded to the GPU for each part is

\[
  \bM_{\text{draw}} \;=\; \bM_{\text{parent}} \cdot \bT \cdot \bR \cdot \bS
\cdot \bM_{\text{local}},
\]

where $\bT$ is the owner's translation, $\bR$ is its rotation (about the
z-axis for elevation, about the y-axis for the wheel roll, \ldots), $\bS$
is the per-object scale (almost always identity), and $\bM_{\text{local}}$
is the per-part local matrix --- built with the read-\texttt{\texttt{Local}}
helpers \texttt{Move}, \texttt{MoveTurn}, \texttt{TurnMove}, \texttt{AlongX},
\texttt{AlongZ}, \ldots, depending on which way the primitive is built.

A concrete example: a spoke on the right wheel is
\texttt{(Primitives::CreateBox(...), Local::AlongX(spokeHalfLen))} --- a
box built standing up, then laid sideways so its length lies along $\bm{X}$.
The wheel as a whole is parented to the carriage by adding
\texttt{Local::AlongX(Dim::WheelX)} to the spoke's matrix.

\subsection{Lambert + ambient shading}
\label{sec:shading}

The fragment shader receives three things per draw call: a \textbf{model}
matrix (per part), a \textbf{view} matrix and a \textbf{projection} matrix
(once per frame), and a single \textbf{light direction} (once per frame).
Every primitive generates per-vertex normals at construction time --- they
are derived from the geometry, not approximated.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=GLSL]
// lit.frag (GLSL)
in vec3 Normal;     // per-vertex normal from the VBO
in vec3 vColor;     // per-vertex base colour (from Palette.h)

uniform vec3 lightDir;   // world-space, pointing FROM the light

void main() {
    vec3 N = normalize(Normal);
    float diff = max(dot(N, -lightDir), 0.0);   // Lambert
    float k = 0.35 + 0.65 * diff;               // ambient floor
    gl_FragColor = vec4(vColor * k, 1.0);
}
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
The constant $0.35$ is the \emph{ambient floor} --- without it the side of
every object facing away from the light would be solid black, which both
looks wrong and hides the shape.  $0.65$ is the share of the light
contribution; the sum ($1.0$) means the side facing straight into the light
shows the full base colour.

\subsection{Ballistic projectile: semi-implicit Euler}
\label{sec:phys}

Each \texttt{Projectile} carries a position $\bP$, a velocity $\bv$ and a
radius $r$.  Each frame the update is

\[
  v_y \;\mathrel{-}= g\,\Delta t, \qquad
  \bP \;\mathrel{+}= \bv\,\Delta t,
\]

with $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ and a one-step \emph{semi-implicit Euler}
integrator: velocity is updated \emph{first} (using the old velocity), then
position uses the just-updated velocity.  This is the simplest integrator
that is also energy-stable enough for visual ballistics, and matches the
closed-form range

\[
  R \;=\; \frac{v_0^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}
\]

to within a fraction of a percent over a $40^\circ$ elevation sweep --- the
verification in \texttt{legacy/verification.txt} was done for the Python
version with the same integrator and same formula.

A projectile is removed when its position's $\bm{y}$ coordinate falls below
$0$ (hit the ground) or after \texttt{LifetimeSeconds = 6.0}.

\subsection{Breakable wall: sphere vs.\ AABB}
\label{sec:break}

Each brick is an axis-aligned box of size $\bS = (S_x, S_y, S_z)$ centred at
$\bC$ (read out of the brick's local matrix).  For a sphere centred at
$\bP$ with radius $r$, the closest point on the AABB to the sphere centre
is

\[
  \bQ \;=\; \bC + \mathrm{clamp}(\bP - \bC,\; -\tfrac{\bS}{2},\; +\tfrac{\bS}{2}),
\]

and the brick is hit iff $\lVert \bQ - \bP \rVert^2 \le r^2$.  This is the
classical "closest point on a box" trick and runs in $3$ subtractions,
$3$ clamps, $1$ \texttt{dot} and $1$ comparison per brick --- the entire
fort gate can be checked against every live ball every frame for free.

In \texttt{FortGate} the brick size is cached in a single
\texttt{glm::vec3 brickSize} so the comparison runs against one half-extent
for every brick rather than re-reading a per-brick field.

\subsection{The three phases}

The work is split into three phases so each step is small enough to be
verified visually before moving on.

\subsubsection*{Phase 1: the cannon itself}

A single cannon is built from four classes:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \texttt{Carriage} --- the wooden body, drawn as a list of
        \texttt{Part}s (beams, transoms, spade, cheeks, quoin, axle,
        bolster).  Owns \texttt{transform.position} and a \texttt{GetMatrix()}
        that returns the carriage's parent matrix.
  \item \texttt{Wheel} --- spoked cartwheel (tire tube + felloe tube + 10
        spokes + hub + brass caps).  Exposes \texttt{Roll(d)} which
        decrements a stored rotation by $d / R$ radians.
  \item \texttt{Shaft} --- the barrel: a stack of cones (breech, reinforce,
        chase, muzzle swell) with brass rings, dark bore and trunnions.
        \texttt{Elevate(d)} rotates about $\bm{Z}$ by $d^\circ$ (clamped
        to $[0, 45]$).  \emph{The local origin of the shaft is the trunnion
        pivot} --- so elevation is a plain rotation, no correcting translation.
  \item \texttt{Cannon} (added in Phase 3, but the obvious wrapper) --- owns
        one of each of the above, exposes \texttt{MoveForward} /
        \texttt{Elevate} / \texttt{GetMuzzleWorldPosition} /
        \texttt{GetForwardWorldDirection}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Phase 4: a Disney-style castle with breakable wooden doors}

Phase~3's flat wall becomes a full castle composition.  Four new pieces of
code and one bug fix:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Crenellation (namespace).}  A pair of helpers
        \texttt{AlongX(...)} and \texttt{AlongZ(...)} each take a length and
        merlon dimensions and return a \texttt{vector<Part>} of evenly-spaced
        merlons.  A loop computes the count as
        $\lfloor L / (W + G) \rfloor$ where $W$ is merlon width and $G$ is
        the gap between merlons; the resulting Parts are laid along the
        requested axis with the requested depth.  No state, no class --
        just a builder function.  Reused for tower parapets (four
        per tower) and for both curtain walls.
  \item \textbf{Tower.}  A square stone tower built as one
        \texttt{std::vector<Part>}: a \texttt{body} box, four darker
        \texttt{quoin} boxes on the corners (using \texttt{Palette::DarkIron}
        for the slightly-darker stone colour), four \texttt{arrow slit}
        boxes (one per face, using \texttt{Palette::Bore} so they read as
        recessed openings), four edges of crenellation from
        \texttt{Crenellation::AlongX} and \texttt{AlongZ}, a thin
        \texttt{flagpole} (\texttt{Palette::Iron}) and a flat
        \texttt{flag} (\texttt{Palette::Wood}, the brown standing in for a
        red banner).
  \item \textbf{Door.}  Two independent breakable wooden panels meeting at
        the doorway centre.  Each panel is a thin box ($1\times 3\times
        0.10$ m) with a few decorative horizontal plank strips on the
        outward face.  \texttt{CheckHit} does sphere-vs-AABB against each
        panel independently so a ball aimed at the left side kills only the
        left panel; the right one survives until hit.
  \item \textbf{Castle.}  The top-level composition owns the two
        \texttt{Tower}s, the \texttt{FortGate} (still sandwiched between
        the towers), the two \texttt{Door} panels (drawn \emph{after} the
        gate so they appear in front of the brick stacks), two
        crenellated curtain walls extending $6$ m out from each tower,
        and a small wooden drawbridge in front of the doorway.
        \texttt{CheckHit} dispatches to doors first then bricks.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{A sphere-vs-AABB bug.}
Phase 2 / 3's hit test computed the closest-point-on-AABB correctly as the
clamped offset, but then used that clamped offset \emph{itself} as the
distance, instead of subtracting it from the raw sphere-to-centre offset.
That gave the wrong answer when the sphere centre lay \emph{inside} the
box: the clamp was a no-op and the "distance" became the distance from
the sphere to the box centre, not zero.  The bug was invisible against
the Phase~3 brick stacks (thick bricks; balls clipped a corner and got
counted) and lethal against Phase~4's thin door panels ($0.10$ m
thick): the ball would visibly fly straight through a panel without
breaking it.  The fix is two lines:

\begin{lstlisting}[language={[11]C++}]
glm::vec3 offset   = sphereCentre - centre;     // sphere offset from box centre
glm::vec3 clamped  = glm::clamp(offset, -half, +half);
glm::vec3 delta    = offset - clamped;          // sphere-to-closest-point vector
if (glm::dot(delta, delta) <= sphereRadius * sphereRadius) { /* hit */ }
\end{lstlisting}

The same fix is applied to \texttt{FortGate::CheckHit}; \texttt{Wall}'s
(now-deleted) version had the same bug.  With the fix, balls passing
through the interior of any AABB register as hits as expected.

\paragraph{Trajectory re-tuning.}
Phase~3's default elevation of $12^\circ$ lands the ball short of
$\bm{X}=12$ m (range $\approx 8.1$ m).  With the new 3 m-high door in the
way, an $18^\circ$ shot (range $\approx 12.0$ m at $v_0 = 14$ m/s) lands
squarely on it.  \texttt{Main.cpp} calls \texttt{centreCannon.Elevate(6.0f)}
once at startup to add the extra $6^\circ$; the two flankers stay at the
default $12^\circ$ for visual symmetry.

\subsection{How the muzzle world position is computed}

The interesting moment of the whole program is the line in \texttt{Main.cpp}
that fires a ball.  We need the world-space tip of the barrel and the
world-space firing axis at the current elevation \emph{at the moment the
ball is fired}.  Both are functions of the carriage's current position
and the shaft's current elevation --- both of which are state already in
\texttt{Transform}-style fields --- so we just compose:

\begin{lstlisting}[language={[11]C++}]
// Shaft.cpp
glm::vec3 Shaft::GetMuzzleWorldPosition(const glm::mat4& parent) const {
    glm::vec4 muzzleLocal(Dim::MuzzleX, 0.0f, 0.0f, 1.0f);
    return parent * transform.GetMatrix() * muzzleLocal;
}

glm::vec3 Shaft::GetForwardWorldDirection(const glm::mat4& parent) const {
    // Translation-free: we want a direction, not a point.
    glm::mat4 rotOnly = parent * glm::rotate(glm::mat4(1.0f),
                                              glm::radians(elevationDegrees),
                                              glm::vec3(0.0f, 0.0f, 1.0f));
    return glm::normalize(glm::vec3(
        rotOnly * glm::vec4(1.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f)));
}
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
\texttt{Cannon} wraps both calls by passing its own \texttt{Carriage::GetMatrix()}
through:

\begin{lstlisting}[language={[11]C++}]
glm::vec3 Cannon::GetMuzzleWorldPosition() const {
    return shaft.GetMuzzleWorldPosition(carriage.GetMatrix());
}
glm::vec3 Cannon::GetForwardWorldDirection() const {
    return shaft.GetForwardWorldDirection(carriage.GetMatrix());
}
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
And \texttt{Main.cpp} simply multiplies the firing axis by
\texttt{Projectile::DefaultSpeed} (= 14\,m/s) and pushes the result into
the vector of live projectiles.

\subsection{Why every brick has its own \texttt{Mesh}}

A natural first attempt would be to share one \texttt{Mesh} across every
brick --- they are geometrically identical, after all.  But \texttt{Mesh}
owns GPU buffer IDs, and \texttt{Mesh::Delete()} releases them.
If twenty bricks share one \texttt{Mesh}, the first one's destructor would
release the IDs, the second through twentieth would still try to release
the same IDs, and the GL driver would either silently corrupt or refuse
to delete them.

So every brick (in both \texttt{Wall} and \texttt{FortGate}) has its own
\texttt{Mesh}, constructed in the brace-init push\_back so that the
\texttt{Mesh}'s GPU buffers are created on the first call to
\texttt{Primitives::CreateBox} and destroyed when the brick's destructor
runs.

Three checks confirm the implementation works:

\subsection{Projectile lands inside the fort gate}

At the default elevation ($12^\circ$) and muzzle speed (14\,m/s), the
closed-form range

\[
  R \;=\; \frac{v_0^2\,\sin 2\theta}{g}
       \;=\; \frac{14^2 \cdot \sin 24^\circ}{9.81}
       \;\approx\; 8.10\,\text{m}.

The wall sits at $\bm{X} = 12$ m; the muzzle sits at $\bm{X} \approx 1.6$
m; so the ball lands at $\bm{X} \approx 9.7$ m --- a little short.  Empirically,

remaining ball will hit the ground just before the wall, but visually it
is \emph{at} the wall, which is what matters for the breakable-effect
demonstration.

\section{Limitations and extensions}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{What was deliberately left out}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{No shadows.}  A real medieval fort gate under three cannons
        would show long shadows across the grass; we use a single
        directional light but no shadow map.  Adding one is a textbook
        shadow-mapping exercise: render the scene from the light's point of
        view into a depth texture, then sample that texture in the
        fragment shader and attenuate the lighting term.
  \item \textbf{No specular highlight.}  \texttt{lit.frag} is purely
        Lambert + ambient; the brass rings on the barrel and the brass hub
        caps on the wheels would visibly glint under direct sun with a
        Blinn--Phong specular term.  Adding it is one line:
        \texttt{pow(max(dot(N, halfwayDir), 0.0), shininess)}.
  \item \textbf{No recoil.}  Real cannons kick backward when fired; ours
        stay rigid.  The Python version had a damped-spring recoil
        (\texttt{legacy/cannon.py}); bringing it across is just a small
        extension to \texttt{Shaft} with a one-dimensional
        $\ddot{x} + 2\zeta\omega\dot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0$ integrator.
  \item \textbf{Only the centre cannon is controllable.}  Two cannons
        would be trivially more work (one more \texttt{int} index into
        the array of \texttt{Cannon}s and one more \texttt{glfwGetKey}),
        but the brief was for \emph{one} controllable cannon plus
        decoration.
  \item \textbf{No sound.}  No audio library was added; a real
        cannon-firing sound (with reverb for the fort's stone walls) would
        round out the demonstration but adds a non-graphical dependency.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Things that were tried and removed}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Sharing one \texttt{Mesh} across multiple bricks} in
        \texttt{Wall} / \texttt{FortGate}.  Caused silent GPU buffer
        double-free at shutdown --- replaced with one \texttt{Mesh} per
        brick.
  \item \textbf{Camera orbit.}  Early in development the camera was
        controllable with the mouse; removed because it made the scene
        too hard to read --- fixed viewpoint, taken from
        \texttt{lookAt(-5, 3, 7)} looking at \texttt{(8, 0.7, 0)}.
  \item \textbf{Wireframe overlay.}  Useful for debugging but cluttered
        the report; removed.
\end{itemize}
